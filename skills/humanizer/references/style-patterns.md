# Punctuation and presentation preferences

Use when adjusting formatting or punctuation. These are deliberate house preferences, not authorship tests.

## Punctuation preferences

- Use no em or en dashes in the rewritten prose. Replace them, and double hyphens used as dashes, with periods, commas, parentheses, a colon that introduces a list or explanation, or a rewritten sentence. A semicolon is not a substitute. Preserve verbatim quotations, proper names, code, and titles when changing them would misquote or corrupt source material; choose prose around them that follows the preference.
- Use a colon before a list, an example, or an explanation the preceding clause promises. Do not use one as a mid-sentence connector that stages a reveal or comparison (“If you're coming from traditional automation: instead of registering handlers, you describe conditions”); state the point directly.
- Prefer straight quotation marks in authored prose. Do not alter the words of a direct quotation.
- Keep attributive compound hyphens; generally remove them in predicate position where the meaning remains clear (“a high-quality report”; “the report is high quality”). Preserve lexical hyphens, including words conventionally always hyphenated such as “third-party” and “cross-functional”, as well as identifiers and names. Do not apply predicate-position cleanup to every hyphenated pair.

## Formatting by rule

- Reduce mechanical boldface, decorative emojis and arrows, bold inline headers, and headings that merely repeat the next sentence. The inline-header tell is a bold label and colon that the line restates (“**Performance:** Performance improved...”). A bold lead-in that names the item, ends in a period, and is followed by new detail (“**Schema in TypeScript.** Tables live in one file.”) can stay. Turn labeled lists into prose only when the labels add no information. Keep lists when they help readers compare or follow steps, and preserve rankings and relationships between items.
- Use bullets for parallel items such as steps, requirements, or options. Turn bullets into prose when the items depend on each other or build an argument, keeping every item and the relationships between them. Give the items in one list the same grammatical form, such as all imperatives or all full sentences.
- Do not use capitals for emphasis, such as an all-caps word or a capitalized common noun (“a TRULY different approach”, “our Network”). Write each product, protocol, and organization name the same way throughout, using its owner's styling (“GitHub”, “iPhone”); when the source is inconsistent and no official form is known, flag the inconsistency rather than pick one.
- Prefer sentence case headings that name what the section contains over headings written only for effect. Replace “The decision, on one screen” with a supported description such as “How the options compare”, not an invented option count. Preserve names and acronyms.
- Remove mechanical horizontal rules between every section and a repeated document title when they serve no structural purpose. Preserve separators required by the format.

## Examples

Before: “The new policy — announced without warning — affects thousands of workers. The changes -- long overdue according to critics -- will take effect immediately.”

After: “The new policy, announced without warning, affects thousands of workers. The changes, long overdue according to critics, will take effect immediately.”

Before: “The update improves performance through optimized algorithms.”

After: “The update uses optimized algorithms to improve performance.”

Do not narrow “performance” to “load times” unless the input supports that claim.
